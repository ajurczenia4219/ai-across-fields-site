Technology: How AI Is Changing Tech Work

What the data says about AI in software, data science, cybersecurity and AI engineering, and whether it helps the people doing the work.

Overview

Artificial intelligence is no longer experimental in the technology industry. McKinsey's 2025 global survey found that 88% of organizations use AI in at least one business function, up from 78% a year earlier. Tech workers were among the first to feel the change, so their experience is an early look at what other fields may face. This page covers seven areas (software development, data science, cybersecurity, AI and machine learning engineering, cloud computing and DevOps, software testing and QA, and UX and product design) and asks one question in each: is AI helping the people who do the work?

Software development

Software is where AI has spread fastest. In Stack Overflow's 2025 survey, 84% of developers said they use or plan to use AI tools, and 51% of professional developers use them daily. The speed gains can be large: in a controlled experiment run by GitHub and Microsoft researchers, developers using Copilot finished a coding task 55% faster. The picture is less simple for experts. A randomized trial by METR found that experienced open-source developers working in their own mature codebases took 19% longer with AI, even though they believed it had made them about 20% faster. Trust is also falling: 46% of developers distrust the accuracy of AI output, while 33% trust it. For new developers, the lesson is that AI speeds up routine work, but reviewing and testing code is now a core skill.

Data science and analytics

In data science, AI mainly shows up in analytics and machine learning workflows. Anaconda's survey of more than 3,000 practitioners found that the top AI use is data cleaning, visualization and analysis (67%), followed by automating tasks and building prediction or detection models (52% each). This matters because preparation has long been the bulk of the job. Anaconda's 2021 survey found data prep and cleaning took 39% of practitioners' time. In analytics, AI now answers questions in plain language and drafts charts and summaries. In machine learning, it supports forecasting, automated model selection and code generation. The labor market remains strong. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects data scientist employment to grow 34% from 2024 to 2034, compared with 3% for all occupations, with a median wage of $112,590. The analyst's value shifts toward asking good questions and checking results.

Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity shows both sides of AI. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach report found that organizations with extensive security AI and automation paid an average of $3.62 million per breach, compared with $5.52 million for those with little or none, a $1.9 million difference. Those organizations also found and contained breaches 80 days faster. Attackers use AI too: 16% of breaches involved attackers using AI tools, most often for phishing and deepfake impersonation. Another 13% of organizations reported a breach of an AI model or application, and 97% of those lacked proper AI access controls. Defenders benefit from the technology, but only if it is governed well.

AI and machine learning engineering

This area is where AI skills themselves are being rewarded. PwC's 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer analyzed nearly a billion job postings and found that jobs requiring AI skills pay an average 56% wage premium over similar roles that do not, up from 25% the year before. Postings requiring AI skills grew 7.5% even as total postings fell 11.3%. Employers' skill requirements are also changing 66% faster in AI-exposed jobs. The takeaway for people entering the workforce is that AI fluency is already valued in pay, and that the skills list is moving quickly.

Cloud computing and DevOps

DevOps and cloud teams run the systems that keep software available, so they were quick to adopt AI for coding, deployment and troubleshooting. Google Cloud's 2025 DORA report surveyed nearly 5,000 technology professionals and found that 90% use AI at work, up 14% from the year before. About 80% reported that AI made them more productive, and respondents spent a median of two hours of their workday with AI tools. Trust lags behind use, though: only 24% said they trust AI output a lot. The report also found that AI works best in teams with strong internal platforms and disciplined engineering practices, so the benefit depends on how well a team is already run.

Software testing and QA

Quality assurance is moving from manual test writing toward AI-assisted test design and monitoring. The World Quality Report 2025-26, from Capgemini, Sogeti and OpenText, found that 89% of organizations are piloting or deploying generative AI in quality engineering, with 37% in production and 52% in pilot. Only 15% have reached enterprise-wide deployment. Organizations reported an average productivity boost of 19%, but about a third saw minimal gains. The biggest barriers were data privacy risks (67%), integration complexity (64%) and hallucination or reliability concerns (60%), and half of respondents said they lack AI and machine learning expertise. For testers, this suggests that people who can judge and supervise AI output are in demand.

UX and product design

Designers are among the heaviest everyday users of AI in tech. In the AI in Design Report 2026, a survey of more than 900 designers, 91% said they use AI multiple times a week or daily, up from 54% the year before, and the average designer's stack grew from three AI tools to seven. Figma's 2025 AI report, which surveyed about 2,500 designers and developers, found a trust gap, with only 32% of designers saying they trust AI output compared with 82% of developers (as reported in secondary coverage). Design work depends on judgment and taste, so AI looks like a way to speed up drafts and routine assets while the human decisions stay with the designer.

Does it benefit the people doing the work?

The evidence suggests yes, with caveats. In a study of 5,179 customer support agents, an AI assistant raised productivity by 14% on average and by 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, and it improved employee retention (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2023). This is a customer service setting, but it shows who may gain most from these tools: newer workers. PwC found that revenue per employee grew 27% in the industries most exposed to AI, versus 9% in the least exposed. The risks are real, though. In McKinsey's survey, 51% of respondents had experienced at least one negative consequence from AI, most often inaccuracy. Only about 6% of companies qualified as AI high performers, so most are still learning how to turn AI into results.

Conclusion

Across the tech industry, AI mostly makes workers faster and more valuable, but the gains depend on skill, task and oversight. For people entering the workforce, three points stand out: learn the fundamentals so you can check AI output, build comfort working with AI tools, and expect your skills to need refreshing often.
